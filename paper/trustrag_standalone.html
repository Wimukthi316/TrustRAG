TrustRAG span-level calibrated hallucination detection for retrieval-augmented generation

Every AI answer reads as if it were true. One line in this one isn't.

When an assistant is handed a document and asked to write from it, the invented part comes out in the same voice as the rest. This system marks the exact words the document does not support, says how sure it is in a number that has been checked against reality, refuses to decide when it cannot, and points at the sentence a person should go and read.

Three answers

One of them is “I don’t know”, and that is the point

A system that always has an opinion is a system you cannot put in front of anything that matters. The middle column is the one most tools will not give you.

Not in the document

The system is willing to make this call on its own. On the specimen above, both flagged passages have no supporting sentence anywhere in the document — which is what an invented claim looks like from the inside.

Can’t tell

The evidence does not separate “supported” from “made up” at the level of care that was asked for, so it goes to a person instead of being guessed. At 90% coverage this is 48% of passages. That cost is measured, printed, and not hidden.

Backed by the document

Left unmarked. Unmarked text is text the system is comfortable with, and drawing a box around the whole answer would say nothing.

Does the number mean what it says

A raw model score is not a probability

Out of the box the detector’s confidence was wrong by 23 points on average. Fitting a correction on held-out data cuts that to 6, so “70%” on screen really does mean about seventy in a hundred. The bottom row is a predictor that ignores its input entirely and always says the same thing — and it wins the first column.

Where the evidence is

Half the time, one sentence out of nineteen

Instead of one guess, the evidence layer returns a set of candidate sentences with a coverage statement attached. On the held-out split it localises the sentence a human annotator actually quoted to 4.6 of 19.1 sentences — and to a single sentence half the time.

At 95% the method returns the entire document, which is it saying plainly that it has nothing useful to offer at that level. The row is printed rather than left out.

The layer can also return nothing. A fifth of the annotators’ own evidence notes are not quotes at all — they say the information simply is not in the source — and recovering that answer scores an F1 of 0.481 against an always-abstain floor of 0.352. A nearest-sentence baseline scores zero here, not because it is weak but because it always returns its nearest sentence and can never say “none”. Per task the picture splits, and one of the three is a loss: see fault 06.

The ledger of failures

What it gets wrong, at the same size as what it gets right

A system that only reports its wins is one you cannot deploy. These are in the paper, in the same type, and none of them was found by a reviewer.

Fault 01

It is beaten by the published detector, and says so

The best public model scores 0.7918 on this task. Ours scores 0.7623, because theirs is a larger model and ours is not. Reproducing their published figure to within 0.04 of a point on our own evaluation code is the evidence that every other number here is measured correctly.

Fault 02

Move it to a different corpus and the promise breaks

At a 90% target, coverage falls from 0.8808 to 0.7410 on a corpus it was not calibrated on. Two repairs were tried and a third was given the answer in advance. None of them fixed it. So the system checks each input at run time and withdraws the promise rather than the detection — which is what the third specimen above is showing.

Fault 03

Half the human evidence quotes are not exact

The people who annotated the benchmark paraphrased the source when they quoted it: only 50.7% of their evidence quotes appear verbatim in the document. Anything scored on string matching is scored against a reference that moves, so the evidence layer scores on content-token containment and prints both columns.

Fault 04

On one task, two trivial policies beat the evidence layer

For writing up a structured record, always returning the longest sentence scores 0.9879 against the real matcher’s 0.9456. The reason is that those documents are serialised data rather than prose, so their “sentences” are an artefact of the serialisation. That is why nothing on this page is quoted as a single pooled average.

Fault 05

On question answering, saying nothing beats the system

73% of the evidence notes on that task say the information is absent, so a policy of always abstaining scores 0.8421. The real abstention layer scores 0.8191. It wins on the other two tasks — 0.3400 against 0.2002, and 0.4865 against 0.4293 — but it loses this one, and the floor is printed beside every task rather than only beside the pooled average, which is how the loss was found.

Fault 06

The cheap first stage throws away a fifth of the answers

A fast embedding filter narrows nineteen candidate sentences to five before the expensive model sees them, and it drops the right sentence 21.9% of the time. A plain word-overlap ranking, scored on the same rows, would have dropped 2.4%. That was the wrong choice, it is measured, and it is reported here rather than quietly re-run.

TrustRAG — span-level calibrated hallucination detection for retrieval-augmented generation. Four components: a span detector, a calibration and conformal layer, a typed explanation layer, and a retrieval-attribution layer. Research prototype, SLIIT IT4010, 2026.

The three specimens on this page were rebuilt from artefacts on disk: the passages and per-token probabilities from the detector’s recorded run, the calibrated score and the verdict from the calibration layer’s own saved calibrator and conformal class, the run-time input check from its own reference, and the evidence from the attribution layer’s own scorer.
